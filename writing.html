Notes from The Most Perpetually Irritated (Short Story)

Click to read the full post...

I don’t mean to brag, but my home town was kind of a utopia. Mai-Opia was “a shining beacon on a hill above a metaphorical moat filled with crocodiles and other unpleasant things” as we self-indulgently described. In truth it was, for most of its history, a sleepy little town situated in a plain quite literally flatter than a pancake, surrounded by more natural resources than it knew what to do with. All major inconveniences in Mai-Opia had been abolished thanks to the visionary leadership of Hal Briggs and the rest of the Municipal Corporation. We had no homelessness, no poverty, and no hunger. We had world-class universal healthcare, a universal basic income, and an extensive public transportation network.

Needless to say, Mai-Opia was far better than wherever you live, and I would have said, about a year ago, that the people in Mai-Opia were better too. They, compared to the worldwide mean, were more generous, kind-hearted, and patient. Mai-Opians knew this fact about themselves, and the Municipal Corporation knew this too. In celebration of this (or because his approval ratings had fallen from 98% to 96%), Hal Briggs launched The Committee of Commonplace Heroism. Each month, The Committee was to choose one neither ordinary, nor extraordinary citizen—“one who falls squarely within the interquartile range of ordinary-ness” was the exact phrase The Committee used. That citizen was to be awarded The Medal of Commonplace Heroism for proving themselves especially cognizant of their ability to improve another Mai-Opian’s day through small, simple actions.

It started off simple. Winners of the medal (which at this time was really just a small ribbon) in the few months of the program included: a teenager who returned her shopping cart during light rain, a man who let other people go first at the stop sign twice, and another man who showed extreme humility when driving, aggressively swerving through multiple lanes of traffic (once nicking the curb) to allow someone to pass him.

By month four, things started to…drift. A man received The Medal (now a full medallion with a certificate inscribed with Hal Briggs’ obnoxiously large signature) for tipping generously after being served a lukewarm cappuccino when he specifically said that it should be “scalding.” Another winner was awarded for smiling patiently through a slow-loading webpage. But the real turning point came when Hornet Hornbey, a mid-level bureaucrat, was honored “for maintaining composure while on hold with customer service for a full seven minutes, before speaking with a polite and efficient representative.” This caused a small uproar, not because Hornbey didn’t deserve The Medal—he very much did, remaining impressively calm and cordial—but because it set a precedent.

Soon, Mai-Opians began to actively seek out minor inconveniences on purpose, hoping to be recognized for their stoicism. A man started a blog about living a week with a pebble in his shoe. Online influences promoted sleeping without a pillow to “connect with ancestral ways.” There were reports of people standing in the longer queue, opting for useless and expensive insurance policies, and temporarily misplacing their keys on purpose, just to feel the uncommon sting of frustration.

The Committee, in an attempt to crack down on what had become an epidemic of inauthenticity, issued a decree stating that acts of commonplace heroism had to be “unmanufactured, spontaneous, and an example of the natural friction of life.” They established a special sub-department to weed out “Deliberate Inconveniencing” and established an elaborate vetting process, requiring a signed affidavit from an unrelated witness to confirm the “heroism.”

Of course, these changes only made things much worse. Mai-Opians began to game the vetting process, forming backdoor deals to witness each other’s inconveniences. A black market trade of forged affidavits sprang up, and Hornet Hornbey was shockingly disgraced when he was caught with a thick binder labeled “Prospective Hardships for Q3.”

By this point, The Committee had a televised ceremony to award The Medal. The Medal itself had evolved, now including a golden medallion, a certificate, a lapel pin, and a (signed) copy of Hal Briggs’ autobiography and magazine.

And all of this, I must remind you, in a town with free housing, no crime, no disease, and ubiquitous access to dog spas and gourmet cat food.

The natural progression of this all was a shift from actual inconveniences (manufactured though they were) to perceived hardships. Once minor suffering became the surest path to social recognition, a new type of race began. The Committee found itself inundated with applications citing all manners of psychological wear and tear. Margaret Maythorpe submitted a 34-page testimonial titled “The Existential Despair of Choosing Between Two Equally Convenient Stores.”

As for most things, this new movement was championed primarily by the dynamic youth, but this time older generations were quick to follow. Mr. Benjamin Roshtow, a news anchor known for his “unflinchingly pragmatic outlook” famously said the constant weather between 70 and 74 degrees and perpetual sunniness felt “a bit…oppressive.”

Eventually, Hal Briggs quietly shut down The Committee for Commonplace Heroism, in its place introducing The Council of Civil Malaise. This is why I said Mai-Opia was, and not is, a utopia: after a few months under The Council’s governance, the primary function of the government was to no longer reduce hardship, but to legitimize it. With the negligence of the Municipal Corporation, public services have deteriorated, and the universal basic income is insolvent. In place of The Medal for Commonplace Heroism came The Medal of Being the Most Perpetually Irritated, which I, much to my bemusement, won shortly before writing this.

I am writing this on a train as I leave Mai-Opia for the neighboring city of Rehyal Meri, a grimy, overcrowded place that seems to be constantly recovering from some flood or earthquake. People have accused me of opportunistically abandoning Mai-Opia, just when the goings get tough. This is not true. The real reason I am leaving is that I saw an article the other day detailing Hornet Hornbey’s current life after his fall from grace. Much to everyone’s shock, Hornbey had moved to Rehyal Meri and was seen in a photograph helping a poor family rebuild their house after it was destroyed by a tornado. He was grinning ear-to-ear.

Why I Want a Blog

Vapidity... Click to read the full post...

It came upon me, while reading Arundhati Roy’s memoir, Mother Mary Comes to Me, that people will write about their lives with an intimacy and rawness that I could not imagine bringing to my own. Why can’t I? Well, it can’t just be the obvious barrier of wanting privacy.

Because that barrier is, luckily for me, amorphous, in that it is not really a true cause for concern. Even in my private journal, where I write in a ridiculous code, there is for the most part nothing but mundane descriptions of the quotidian, surface-level fears and desires about school and so on. In what should be a sanctuary of self-expression and abandonment, I cannot bring myself to write about anything cerebral. I go so far as to behave as if I am writing to someone, to an archaeologist in the future, maybe. So, my closest guarded secrets will never be words on a page and will remain just thoughts. It is confounding that in what is doubtless the safety of my private journal, I still write as if being offered up to judgment; nothing can be a true expression of myself.

Can such a thing exist? A person’s personhood in its purest form? Is there anything to a person that is not the influence of other people and society? Using myself as an example, I would say there is no such thing. I try to pull back the various layers and constructions of external images and personalities only to find more, like a never-ending artichoke. I am saying that I am a phony. There is no heart to this artichoke. I believe I am not alone in this. I feel, in a diluted and much less tragic way, like the protagonist of Osamu Dazai’s novel, No Longer Human. My affliction is far more normal and ordinary.

Maybe the reason for my affliction is that I think in words. My thoughts make up a continuous speech spoken only to myself. Sometimes, I can see the words scrolling across my surroundings like subtitles, or, more aptly, a teleprompter. I learned, to my shock, that some people do not think like that. Their thoughts are in another form, or formless entirely.

Maybe my lack of heart is because that language, the medium of my thoughts, immediately invites itself to suspicion. I think it is the constant cross-referencing and judgment of my own thoughts by my own self that produces the multitude of layers that collectively form the person writing this.

Have I contradicted myself? I don’t think so. Most of what I write—and think for that matter—is utterly vapid, derivative, and lacking any identifiable form of originality.

Anyway, let me return to the original point: why I want a blog. I am in the unique position of being able to blur the lines between my journal and blog. I, unlike Arundhati Roy, can safely put my thoughts online without sharing them with millions. In fact, I am really like the Voyager satellite: broadcasting a message to an empty and silent universe. Last month, three people visited this website, and I am pretty sure all three were me.

But, of course, there are benefits to publishing for the public. For one, since I have an image to maintain, it requires a bare minimum of polish and coherence. Otherwise, I would simply be scribbling a few questions in my journal, and that would be the end of what could possibly be an illuminating rambling session (this is yet to be one). Then there is, of course, the highly unlikely chance that someone besides me or outside of my immediate family (even that is pretty unlikely) reads this, and the even more unlikely chance that they find it interesting.

I would anyway type something like this (I’m not writing by hand); after it is written, the comparative effort to publish it is very small. Just some mathematical hedging to take those chances.

If you—yes, you—are not me, I would much appreciate your feedback. My email is on the upper left.

Book Review: The Order of Time

Click to read the full review...

The Order of Time by Carlo Rovelli
My rating: 3 of 5 stars

In my mind, nonfiction books struggle to find the balance between being technical and substantive while remaining accessible to the average reader. One book that achieves this balance is Guns, Germs, and Steel. Incognito by David Eagleman errs on the side of being too easy to grasp, sacrificing substantivity as a result, while Black Holes: The Key to Understanding the Universe by Brian Cox and Jeff Forshaw errs to the other side, making me feel completely out of my depth while reading it. I sought to finally find a science-related book that finds this balance, but unfortunately, I was disappointed again with The Order of Time.

It definitely leans toward being easy to grasp. Although it never promises to be a comprehensive overview of the physics behind time, I nonetheless found it insufficiently technically engaging. I would say there are only around two chapters whose focus is technical; the rest are more concerned with “the ideas” and their implications. There are certainly interesting ideas being discussed. Namely, I found it illuminating how Rovelli makes the point that the direction and flow of time is only determined by entropy, specifically its never-ending increase in the universe.

I enjoyed the sprinkling of references throughout the book to everything from the Bible and the Mahabharata to Bach. I would say it is a bit pedantic, but that is the type of thing I would do if I ever wrote a book.

Rovelli tries to bridge the gap between physics and philosophy. Unfortunately, he does not succeed in this mission. Particularly in the final chapter, which is something of a broader reflection on the nature of time and humanity’s relationship with it, the ideas wander far too much, and any discernable point became muddled.

This is mostly due to Rovelli’s usage of language, which is more often than not vague as a result of excess ornamentation. As you can imagine, this is quite a problem in a physics book attempting to discuss such abstract ideas. I feel I left with something of an idea of many interesting topics, but very little actual information.

Book Review: Swann’s Way

Click to read the full review...

Swann’s Way by Marcel Proust
My rating: 5 of 5 stars

Before anything else, I must admit that I did not nearly absorb all that this book has to offer; and I will no doubt return to Swann's Way, and Proust in general. It was also much more difficult to read than I initially thought.

Obviously, the book is extremely well-written. The sentences are long, flowing with wonderful descriptions and turns of phrase. I read that Proust's original French prose was not so ornamental, but I don't really mind it. I wouldn't say that prose is dense; I think the difficult of the book comes from its length and keeping track of the extremely detailed characterization Proust establishes for each person.

When reading about the discussions of Swann's Way online, I saw that many people say that the book is about time. I would humbly disagree (I recognize the full novel is called In Search of Lost Time, but hear me out).

I think the brilliance of this book is that it is as close the written word can get to representing the true, layered (and erratic) way humans think. It shows that how we see the world is in fact not accurate at all, and is really our own construction based off of our desires and memories. We see this in the way the narrator reflects on his childhood in Combray, or how Swann idealizes Odette. In both cases, the reality is far from the character's mental construction of it.

And the beauty of the book's focus: human thought, is that it is truly universal. Indeed, I have been wondering about some of the themes of the book long before I ever read or heard of it. Namely, I've wondered how we can attach such visceral emotion and memory to the most inconsequential of tangible and intangible objects. In the narrator's case, it is the taste of madeleine and tea, and in my case, the smell of mothballs and gasoline.

This idea is essentially the same as that scene in Ratatouille. When Anton Ego eats the ratatouille, he is transported back in time to his mother cooking for him when he was a child. That scene is taken straight from Proust.

Musings on Bach

Some thoughts and advice on playing and listening to Bach. Click to read the full post...

Click to hear Bach’s Prelude and Fugue in B-flat minor, no. 22, BWV 867 from The Well-Tempered Clavier, played by Chris Breemer. Sourced from the IMSLP.

Note: The following is a mix of fact and opinion. I trust the discerning reader to tell the difference between the two.

Johann Sebastian Bach is considered by many to be the greatest composer of Western classical music[i], including me (or at least I would say the debate is between him and Beethoven). And if we wish to maintain a façade of objectivity, there is a preponderance of evidence to support the fact. Bach has a massive corpus of work, spanning more than 1,100 surviving pieces and 175 hours[ii]; he, according to Classic FM, had an IQ of 165 (calculated retrospectively)[iii].He fathered an unparalleled twenty children, five of whom were also named Johann, and four of whom went on to become famous composers in their own right (whom I collectively refer to as “the lesser Bachs”)[iv].

He is heralded as a master, really the master, of counterpoint[v]. The title “master of” is applied to several composers in different contexts; Franz Schubert is often called a master melodist[vi]. However, one can more objectively (continuing the façade) be a master of counterpoint than melody, although I will argue later on that Bach, too, is a master melodist. What makes a melody masterful is far less clear than what makes counterpoint so. A master of counterpoint is more akin to a mathematician than a great painter, because the rules of counterpoint are as precise as they are unflinching[vii]. For proof, I suggest you take a look at this series of excellent tutorials by Jacob Gran. From this, you will see that there is sometimes an objectively right note, or, more likely, an objectively wrong one. That is not to say that there is no creativity needed in counterpoint. On the contrary, as Jacob Gran notes, masterful counterpoint comes from subverting the rules, which Bach does brilliantly. He uses startling dissonances to build tension, then returns to consonance to resolve it in a satisfying way. As an example, here is the end of the prelude from Prelude and Fugue No. 22 in B-flat minor, BWV 867[viii]:

We’ve discussed Bach’s mastery of counterpoint, but most would agree that counterpoint is a necessary but insufficient part of being a great composer. What about his music overall? To me, one attribute defines it above all else, whether listening or playing: the sense of many parts converging into something greater than their sum. His pieces build toward a culmination in which the motifs, melodies, and harmonies from earlier return, and one gets the sense of a grand design finally revealed, wherein every puzzle piece is placed correctly. This is felt most profoundly with pieces like Passacaglia in C minor, BWV 582, or Prelude and Fugue in E-flat major “St. Anne’s”, BWV 552. Bach has the reputation of being overly technical, a bit stuffy, but, while it is true that knowledge of music theory and the structure of his pieces will enhance the listening experience[ix], his music also tends to be inherently pleasing to the ears; you can, if you have the inclination, simply let it wash over you.

I have a confession to make. What prompted me to write this was not a sudden, inspirational love of Bach; rather, it was frustration. I was trying to learn the fugue from Prelude and Fugue No. 22 (one of the easiest fugues from The Well-Tempered Clavier). It, like all his pieces, starts off innocently, almost as a taunt: a simple melody in the right hand, which is then repeated in the left hand. As one glances across the page, there is nothing that immediately jumps out as extremely difficult, but be warned: there will be a singular bar a couple of lines in that can only be called a special brand of torture, violating the Eighth Amendment. Playing it requires superhuman dexterity, since Bach insists that some note be held for its exact duration so the voicing is preserved. But rest assured, that bar can be played. No, no, no! This is not some lesser composer who occasionally writes a chord that can only be played with hands the size of Rachmaninoff’s (**cough cough** Alkan, Scriabin, etc…).

Here is the passage that can be blamed for the above outburst:

Not so bad, right? Look again, dear reader! Measures 15–18 are that special brand of torture I was referring to. And it certainly doesn’t help that the editor has given no fingerings. In measure 18, because the B-flat must be held for three beats, and Bach demands that the G-flat be held for four, you must contort your hand to reach the E-flat, a stretch which I was just barely able to do.

Ah, I must have bored you by now with my venting. A brief note on learning Bach more generally: progress at the beginning is intolerably slow. You will wade through the piece, practicing hands independently for what seems like far too long. But one day, you sit down to practice and find that you are no longer searching for the notes. You are simply playing, with seeming effortlessness. Improvement when practicing the music of other composers is more or less linear (maybe logarithmic), but improvement with Bach is discontinuous! You will suddenly be able to play the piece and laugh at your former suffering. Just as with listening, the parts will seem to come together on their own, and you will be left with a complete creation.

Now that I’ve discussed (or rather complained) about playing Bach in some depth, I want to explore two aspects of his music that are slightly overlooked: his beautiful melodies and his capacity to provoke emotion. Firstly, you will find a bounty of delightful, sorrowful, and lyrical melodies in his repertoire. Take the opening of the prelude referred to earlier:

It is a haunting, almost aching melody that no doubt should prove to you (assuming, dear reader, you are of average or below stubbornness) Bach’s brilliance as a melodist. Need more evidence? There are a plethora of examples, but here are some of the most famous: Air on the G String, Prelude in C major from Cello Suite No. 1, Badinerie from Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor, Aria from the Goldberg Variations.

Many of the most emotionally potent moments of my life involve music. I am reminded of a specific moment during a trip to Iceland. We were driving somewhere in the evening (I’ve forgotten the specifics), surrounded by the stark Icelandic scenery: towering mountains on one side, a barren volcanic wasteland on the other. I was listening to Rachmaninoff’s Second Piano Concerto, allowing myself to be lifted by those beautiful melodies, when suddenly, partly because I was in an uncharacteristically sentimental mood, I was overcome by a feeling of gratitude and awe. Gazing upon my surroundings, I was taken aback, shaken.

Yes, a bit saccharine, I know, but is this not the purpose of music? To reveal beauty and to honor God’s creation? Or in my case, as an atheist, to recognize the world’s splendor, its innate exceptionalism, and to prompt action? The contrarian reader would point to the fact that I was listening to Rachmaninoff, not Bach. I would reply that had I been listening to the Chaconne from Partita No. 2 in D minor, the same effect would have been produced, if not with greater potency. Later in that trip, that same feeling came over me, this time courtesy of Bach, when listening to the Prelude from Cello Suite No. 4, a rather unassuming piece which I had never previously listened to thoughtfully. On the famously austere pages of Bach, which might appear like an endless sea of sixteenth notes, an emotional blueprint lies in its entirety. Despite there being no markings to instruct dynamic or phrasing choice, a skillful player will reveal the hidden nuances and bring to life phrasing that in retrospect seems inevitable. Listen to the Chaconne, or an organ fugue if you’re feeling adventurous. Allow yourself to be swept away, and you will understand my effusiveness. Such is the brilliance of Bach.

Book Review: Blindness

A review of José Saramago's dystopian allegory about sight and human moral blindness. Click to read the full review...

Blindness by José Saramago
My rating: 3 of 5 stars

"Blindness" is a post-apocalyptic novel in which a sudden pandemic of "white blindness" spreads through a city. The government initially confines the infected in a mental asylum, but soon no one remains uninfected except a character known only as “the doctor’s wife,” who retains her sight.

Saramago writes in an avant-garde, stream-of-consciousness style, with minimal punctuation, few paragraph breaks, and blurring of dialogue and narration. I was initially concerned that the style would pose difficulty, but that proved unfounded. In fact, it was effective and immersive with several poignantly written lines. An example, which is essentially the "thesis" of the book:

"I don't think we did go blind, I think we are blind, Blind but seeing, Blind people who can see, but do not see. ... The doctor's wife got up and went to the window ... Then she lifted her head up to the sky and saw everything white, It is my turn she thought. Fear made her quickly lower her eyes. The city was still there" (Saramago 326).


The middle section of the novel is extremely slow-paced and claustrophobic. It is set entirely within a single ward of the asylum. The small cast of characters remains nameless. This is where the plot stalls the most, and the condition the internees endure is described with painful detail. Of course, these choices are deliberate on Saramago's part, meant to immerse the reader in the confined existence of the blind.

The novel carries two thematic threads. The first is the titular blindness which exists even before the pandemic. Saramago's suggests that the social structures we maintain function largely because we agree not to see certain things: cruelty, poverty, the suffering immediately around us. The epidemic does not introduce a new blindness; it simply removes the layers that made the existing one comfortable and invisible. The second thread follows from this: given that removal, what remains of the human spirit? Saramago's answer is unsentimental. The thugs who seize control of the asylum represent one truth of human nature, while the small group around the doctor's wife, who bears the burden of "seeing," represents another.

My main criticism is that the thematic payoff at the end does not feel entirely worth the journey. While it is earned, it is ultimately a familiar message that lacks the originality or force needed to justify the prolonged stagnation of the middle section. "Blindness" is an admirable book, but not a rewarding one.

Book Review: The Book of Disappearance

A critical reflection on Ibtisam Azem's novel exploring the Palestinian relationship to their homeland and the challenges of cultural change. Click to read the full review...

The Book of Disappearance by Ibtisam Azem
My rating: 3 of 5 stars

The following is not a real review, but more a critical reflection on the novel's politics.

I can say that "The Book of Disappearance" at least prompted the correct questions in me: what is the meaning of a place? how does one react to the inevitable change in culture? whose role is it to adapt?

This book attempts to explore the importance of the Palestinian people to the modern-day state of Israel. It suggests that the Palestinian people have a unique relationship with their homeland, one that many Israelis struggle to fully understand.

One of the main protagonists of the novel is a Palestinian man, who, inheriting much of the nostalgia and bitterness of his grandmother, feels a profound sense of loss living in the modern state of Israel.

Ordinarily, in this debate, my support rests firmly with the Palestinians, unusually, however, reading this novel, I felt myself disagreeing with the Palestinian point of view.

It is one thing to acknowledge the suffering that displacement and violence have caused the Palestinian people. It is another to suggest that a particular people possess an exclusive and immutable relationship to a place. Cultural change, migration, and the transformation of cities are nearly universal historical experiences. Languages fade, neighborhoods change, and identities evolve. The protagonist seems unable to imagine identity outside of memory and loss, clinging to a vision of place that cannot survive historical change. My fear is that such thinking leads to rigid communalism, where unchanging, inherited identity is the determining factor in all of life.

What makes the Palestinian case morally urgent is the violence and dispossession that accompany it, not the mere fact of cultural transformation itself. At times the novel seemed to blur this distinction, presenting cultural loss as something uniquely Palestinian rather than a common feature of human history.
